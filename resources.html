AI for students

A working list · last updated July 2026

I have spent the last two years using AI to study, build websites and apps, run marketing for a manufacturing business, and start a company. Most of what I learned came from getting it wrong repeatedly. This page is the version I wish someone had handed me at the start.

It is a working document. If something here is out of date or wrong, tell me.

The one thing that matters most

AI does not think for you. It responds to you. If you do not know what you are asking, you get nothing useful back, and worse, you get something that looks useful. Every word in a prompt is a decision: the framing, the context, the constraints, the tone. Change one and the output changes completely.

The gap between someone who uses AI and someone who understands it is much wider than most people admit. It is closed by iteration, not by finding a magic prompt.

How to actually prompt

  • Give it a role and an audience. "Explain this to a Grade 11 Economics student" produces something different from "explain this."
  • Give it the constraints up front. Length, format, what to avoid, what it must include. Vague in, vague out.
  • Paste the source material. Don't make it recall, make it read. Accuracy rises sharply.
  • Ask it to show its reasoning when the answer matters, so you can check the steps rather than trusting the conclusion.
  • Iterate rather than restart. Tell it what was wrong with the last output. Most people start a new chat when they should have given one line of feedback.
  • Ask it to argue against itself. The fastest way to find the weak part of your own thinking.

Where it fails

A Harvard and BCG study found that AI made average consultants perform like experts on some tasks, and made them confidently wrong on tasks just outside that zone. The boundary is invisible and unlabelled. I wrote about this in Jagged.

Practically, for a student: it invents citations, it is unreliable on very recent events, it will agree with you when you are wrong, and it is weakest exactly where your subject knowledge is weakest, which is the moment you are least able to catch it. Verify anything you would be embarrassed to get wrong.

Using it honestly

If you are in the IB or any programme with academic integrity rules, the line matters and it is not blurry. Using AI to understand a concept, structure your thinking, find sources, or get feedback on something you wrote is learning. Submitting text you did not write as your own is not, and both the IB and most universities require you to declare authorship.

The practical test I use: could I explain and defend every sentence in this if someone asked me about it right now? If not, I have not finished the work.

Tools I actually use

Short list on purpose. Most “best AI tools” posts are affiliate links for products nobody opens twice. These are the ones I return to.

  • ChatGPT and Claude are the core of everything. Thinking through problems, drafting, restructuring, arguing with. I use both because they fail differently, and comparing two answers is often faster than checking one.
  • Perplexity for anything that needs a source. It cites where it got things, which makes it the right tool when I am researching rather than writing, and the wrong tool when I need something built.
  • Grok for a fast second read and for what people are actually saying about something right now, rather than what was true last year.
  • Notion holds everything else. Notes, business plans, research, task ownership for the family business. If it is not written down somewhere I will look again, it did not happen.
  • Google Calendar because none of the above matters if the work never gets a time slot. Most of my planning problems turned out to be scheduling problems.

If you’re building something

Most students who want to build something never start, because the first version they imagine is the finished one. Here is the order that actually works.

  • Start with a problem you have watched someone have. Not a market gap you read about. I built BuildSmart because I spent years watching my father run a good business without the roadmap he needed. That is a stronger foundation than a clever idea.
  • Build the smallest version that does one useful thing. Not a platform. One feature that works. You learn more from a week of someone using a rough version than from a month of planning a polished one.
  • Learn the domain, not just the tool. AI will build you a website in an afternoon. It will not tell you whether anyone wants it. The judgment is the part you have to own, and it is the part that compounds.
  • Expect the boring middle. Setup is fast and fun, launch is fast and fun, and the ninety percent in between is fixing things that keep breaking for reasons you do not understand yet. That stretch is where most projects die.
  • Ship before you are ready. Not carelessly, but a real version in front of one real user beats a perfect version in front of nobody. Every business I have worked on improved faster after contact with someone who did not care about the idea, only about whether it worked.
  • Keep a record of what went wrong. The failures are the actual education. Anyone can copy the outcome. Nobody can copy two years of knowing why the obvious approach does not work.
