Is AI Obsoleting Business Consulting, or Reinventing It?

Forthcoming

Independent research paper · publication in progress

Abstract

For most of the past century, business consulting ran on a simple advantage: firms like McKinsey and BCG knew things their clients did not, and clients paid a premium for access to that knowledge. Generative AI is now producing much of that knowledge in minutes, and the industry is visibly reorganizing around that fact. This paper asks whether AI is making business consultants obsolete or creating a new kind of consulting profession. Using secondary research, it draws on a field experiment involving 758 BCG consultants published in Organization Science (Dell’Acqua et al., 2026), a bibliometric review of 195 academic publications, recent Harvard Business Review scholarship on consulting firm structure, and industry reporting on McKinsey, BCG, and Bain from 2023 to 2026. The paper finds that both answers are true at once, for different tiers of the profession. The analytical work that once justified large teams of junior consultants is being automated inside the major firms themselves, while demand for judgment-heavy work, particularly AI implementation and governance, is growing fast enough to reshape firm structures around it. The paper also finds that AI degrades consultant performance on tasks outside its capability range, which makes knowing where that boundary sits the most defensible consulting skill of the next decade.

Keywords artificial intelligence · management consulting · knowledge work · jagged technological frontier · consulting obelisk

Why this question

Every profession rests on a scarcity. Doctors on diagnosis, lawyers on precedent, consultants on knowing what has already worked somewhere else. For a century that last scarcity held. A firm could accumulate what it learned across hundreds of clients, and sell access to that accumulation at a premium, and the premium was fair, because the knowledge was real and genuinely hard to obtain any other way.

What interests me is what happens to a profession when the scarcity it was built on disappears but the profession itself does not. That is not really a technology question. It is a question about what clients were paying for all along, and whether the people selling it always knew. If the answer turns out to be judgment rather than information, then the last century of consulting was priced on the wrong thing, and the industry is only now being forced to find out what it was actually worth.

Consulting is a useful place to ask this because it is unusually exposed. Its product is pure knowledge work, its economics are public, and its pyramid structure makes the effects of automation legible in a way most industries can hide. What happens there over the next decade is a preview of what happens to every profession that sells expertise, which, for anyone deciding what to study now, is not an abstract concern.
